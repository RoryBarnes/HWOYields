\title{\bfseries HWO ExoEarth Yields for a Mass-Selected Planet Sample\\
in a Narrowed Habitable Zone\\[0.3em]
\large A rederivation of the AYO yield model of Stark et al., laid out as a probabilistic model}
\author{Prepared in the \texttt{yields} vaibify project}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
How many exoEarth candidates (EECs) would the baseline Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO)
mission of Stark et al.\ (2024) find if the definition of an exoEarth were changed? Stark's
canonical definition counts planets smaller than $1.4\,R_\oplus$ in a habitable zone of
$0.95$--$1.67$~AU, scaled by $\sqrt{L_\star}$. We instead count planets of $0.5$--$2\,M_\oplus$
(with $R = M^{0.27}$) in a narrower zone of $0.96$--$1.20$~AU, scaled the same way. Because the
survey redistributes its observing time whenever the number of planets it expects to find changes,
the answer cannot be obtained by rescaling Stark's yield; the survey optimization must be run
again.

We rederive the Altruistic Yield Optimization (AYO) model from its published equations and
parameters, follow Stark's documented methods wherever they are stated, and write the model as a
probabilistic graphical model whose only decision is how the survey allocates its time. Nothing is
fitted to Stark's results. The model's expected 6\,m yield is 20.92, against a published
22.5. The median canonical yield is 18.8 (90\% interval 5.6--53.4), and the
median redefined yield is 4.3 (1.2--14.8). Their ratio,
$0.232^{+0.027}_{-0.021}$ (68\% interval), exceeds the occurrence ratio of $0.204$
because the narrower zone discards the faintest planets, and it stays between 0.2201
and 0.2386 across seven model variants. The chance of finding at least 25 candidates falls from 35.1\% to
1.20\%.

Of 24 independent comparisons with the published record, 21 agree. Two
disagreements remain, and each reduces to a question that only the AYO authors can answer. First,
the time needed to take a planet's spectrum depends more steeply on target difficulty and on
aperture than in Stark's model, and a 56-point study shows that no setting of the
throughput parameters removes the difference. Second, the published uncertainty interval on
$\eta_\oplus$ admits two readings, and that single choice accounts for 99.1\% of the
remaining disagreement with Stark's yield distribution. Stark's own albedo test, adopted here,
gives an albedo penalty of 3.6\% rather than the 12\% he reports.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Yield estimates for HWO rest on the AYO framework (Stark et al.\ 2014, 2019, 2024). AYO allocates
exposure time across a target list by an equal-slope rule, so its output responds to a change in
the planet population in ways a proportionality argument does not capture: when fewer detections
are expected, characterization time is freed and the survey reallocates it. A question about a
different planet population therefore requires the survey to be re-optimized.

This report answers one such question. We ask how many planets the baseline HWO mission would find
around FGK stars if
\begin{itemize}
\item planets were selected by mass, $0.5$--$2\,M_\oplus$ with $R = M^{0.27}$ (Chen \& Kipping
2017), and
\item the habitable zone were $0.96$--$1.20$~AU for the Sun, scaled as $\sqrt{L_\star}$ for other
stars.
\end{itemize}
Throughout, Stark's selection is called the \emph{canonical box} and ours the \emph{redefined
box}. AYO's code is not public, so we reimplement the model from the published equations and
parameter tables (Secs.~\ref{sec:model} and~\ref{sec:nodes}), validate it against every published
number we can reach (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}), and then evaluate both boxes
(Sec.~\ref{sec:results}). Section~\ref{sec:variants} measures how much the answer depends on
settings that the published record leaves open, and Sec.~\ref{sec:open} describes the one
discrepancy we cannot remove.

\section{The model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{The model as an influence diagram}

AYO is usually described as a procedure: compute completeness, optimize exposure times, count
planets, then add sources of uncertainty one at a time. That description hides the structure that
determines the result: which quantities are random and which are fixed, and which random
quantities the survey planner can see when it decides how to spend its time. Stark et al.\ (2024,
Sec.~3) call the last distinction \emph{actionable} versus \emph{static} uncertainty. A graphical
model makes it precise.

Figure~\ref{fig:pgm} shows the model as an influence diagram: a directed acyclic graph of random
variables with a single \emph{decision} node, the survey allocation. Table~\ref{tab:nodes}
describes each node and the pipeline step that implements it.

\subsection{The joint distribution, and how it is marginalized}
\label{sec:joint}

With the fixed quantities $(\mathcal{M}, \mathcal{B}, \{\mathbf{s}_i\})$ held constant, the graph
factorizes as
\begin{multline}
p(\eta_\oplus, \alpha, \beta, \mathbf{z}, \mathbf{N}, \mathbf{o}, \mathbf{A}, Y)
= p(\eta_\oplus)\,p(\alpha,\beta)\prod_{i} p_z(z_i)\,
\mathrm{Poisson}\!\left(N_i;\ \eta_\mathcal{B}\right) \\
\times \prod_{j=1}^{N_i} p(\mathbf{o}_{ij} \mid \alpha, \beta, \mathcal{B}, \mathbf{s}_i)\,p_A(A_{ij})
\cdot \delta\!\Big(Y - \sum_{ij} D_{ij}\Big),
\label{eq:joint}
\end{multline}
where the allocation enters $D_{ij}$ through the deterministic function
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_i = \mathbf{a}_i\!\left(\lambda(\eta_\mathcal{B}, \mathbf{C}),\ C_i\right),
\qquad C_i = C_i(\mathbf{s}_i, z_i, \mathcal{M}, \mathcal{B}, \alpha, \beta).
\label{eq:alloc}
\end{equation}

Two properties make this tractable.

\textbf{Poisson thinning.} Once the allocation is fixed, each planet is counted independently,
with a probability that depends only on its host star, so the planet draw inside the plate can be
marginalized exactly. If $N_i \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\eta_\mathcal{B})$ and each planet is counted
with probability $\bar{C}_i$ (the completeness at the allocated exposure, averaged over albedo),
then the number of counted planets around star $i$ is
$\mathrm{Poisson}(\eta_\mathcal{B}\bar{C}_i)$, and the survey total is
\begin{equation}
Y \mid \eta_\oplus, \mathbf{z} \ \sim\ \mathrm{Poisson}\!\Big(\Lambda(\eta_\oplus, \mathbf{z})\Big),
\qquad
\Lambda = \eta_\mathcal{B} \sum_i \bar{C}_i\!\left(\mathbf{a}_i(\eta_\mathcal{B}, \mathbf{z})\right).
\label{eq:thinning}
\end{equation}
Step A07 evaluates $\Lambda$ at 24 values of $\eta_\oplus$, \emph{re-optimizing the survey at
each one}, interpolates between them, and draws one Poisson count for each of the 14,400
posterior samples of $\eta_\oplus$. This is Stark's construction: in his Sec.~3.1 he draws a
Poisson number of planets for each star and counts those with a valid record of visits.

\textbf{Albedo is static.} The planner allocates time using $C_i$ computed for a fixed albedo,
$A_G = 0.2$. The yield is then counted from the same planets and orbits with each planet's albedo
$A_{ij}$ drawn at random, using Stark's per-visit test (Sec.~\ref{sec:albedo}). A planet darker
than planned may go undetected in the fixed allocation. Because there is no path from $A_{ij}$
into $\mathbf{a}_i$, the albedo penalty is a consequence of the graph, not an added correction.

\textbf{What is marginalized.}
\begin{itemize}
\item $\eta_\oplus$: marginalized, with the survey re-optimized for each value, because it is
actionable.
\item $N_i$, $\mathbf{o}_{ij}$, $A_{ij}$: marginalized exactly by thinning,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:thinning}.
\item $\alpha, \beta$: marginalized for the \emph{ratio} $\rho_\mathcal{B}$; the planet population
inside each box uses the SAG13 central values.
\item $\mathbf{z}$: marginalized over 200 joint draws (Stark uses 500), with the survey
\emph{re-optimized for every draw}, because exozodi is actionable. Step A04 tabulates each star's
completeness at 23 exozodi levels (0--1024 zodis), and each draw interpolates between them; against
direct evaluation at the drawn levels, this changes the optimized yield by at most
0.24\%. Each posterior sample of $\eta_\oplus$ is paired with one draw of $\mathbf{z}$.
At fixed $\eta_\oplus$, the yield averaged over draws is 17.73, with a draw-to-draw scatter
of 0.57.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Actionable and static uncertainty}

A source of uncertainty is \emph{actionable} if there is a directed path from its node to the
decision node, and \emph{static} if there is not. In Fig.~\ref{fig:pgm}:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\eta_\oplus \to \eta_\mathcal{B} \to \lambda \to \mathbf{a}_i$: actionable. A mission that
finds more planets must spend more time characterizing them, so the yield-maximizing survey depends
on $\eta_\oplus$. This is why yield grows more slowly than $\eta_\oplus$, and why the redefined
yield cannot be obtained by rescaling.
\item $z_i \to C_i \to \mathbf{a}_i$: actionable. Exozodi is measured on the first visit, and the
survey adapts to it (Stark et al.\ 2015).
\item $A_{ij}$, $N_i$, $\mathbf{o}_{ij}$: static. The planner cannot know which stars host
planets, where those planets are, or how dark they are.
\end{itemize}
Each observational penalty in this problem (the albedo penalty, the exozodi penalty, the difference
between Stark's red and purple curves in his Fig.~10) is a statement about which of these paths
exist.

\section{Model components}
\label{sec:nodes}

\subsection{Stars}

The target list is the HWO Preliminary Input Catalog (HPIC; Tuchow et al.\ 2024), which Stark et
al.\ (2024) use. It contains 11,757 dwarf stars within 50\,pc, 7,767 of them FGK. Each
star's photon flux is computed from a blackbody at its catalogued $T_{\rm eff}$ and $R_\star$.
Compared with the measured $V$ magnitudes of 674 stars in HPIC, the median residual between
5300 and 7300\,K is +0.019\,mag, and the fluxes agree with the Pickles spectral templates used
by EXOSIMS to a ratio of 0.979. The survey chooses from the 1,500 stars whose habitable
zones are widest in units of $\lambda/D$, after a noise-floor screen that depends on the box being
searched.

\subsection{Occurrence: $\eta_\oplus$}
\label{sec:eta}

Stark et al.\ (2024, Sec.~3.5) derive $\eta_\oplus$ by integrating the Kepler DR25 posteriors of
Bryson et al.\ (2021) over their canonical domain, drawing equally from Bryson's two bounding cases
for extrapolating completeness, and quote $0.26^{+0.29}_{-0.14}$ as ``mean and 86\% confidence
interval''. Bryson's posterior chains are not published, so the distribution must be
reconstructed.

We use a lognormal and set its width from Stark's own Fig.~10, which shows the yield distribution
with $\eta_\oplus$ fixed (red curve, mean 17.35) and with $\eta_\oplus$ uncertain (purple
curve). Holding this model's yield-versus-$\eta_\oplus$ relation at the level of the red curve,
the lognormal that best turns the red curve into the purple one has median 0.26 and
$\ln$-width 0.80, reproducing the purple curve to a total-variation distance of
0.019. Reading the quoted interval as 86\% gives a $\ln$-width of 0.52, a
distance of 0.142, and a peak at 15 instead of the published 10.
Reading it as a one-sigma (68\%) interval gives a $\ln$-width of 0.76, a distance of
0.024, and a peak at 12. Bryson's Fig.~14, digitized from its vector
source, shows each extrapolation case with a $\ln$-width of about 0.8, consistent with Stark's
figure and not with the 86\% reading. We therefore read the interval at $z = 1$
(Table~\ref{tab:nodes}).

This choice departs from Stark's text in two places: the interval is read as 68\% rather than
86\%, and the lognormal is centered on 0.26, which makes 0.26 its median, whereas Stark calls 0.26
the mean (the model's mean $\eta_\oplus$ is 0.343). Step A15 tests the interval against its
own source without using the yield model. It mixes Bryson's two cases equally, as Stark describes,
and rescales the mixture so that its mean is 0.26. That distribution puts 59\% of its
probability inside the quoted $[0.12, 0.55]$, much closer to 68\% than to 86\%; its own 86\%
interval, $[0.047, 0.653]$, is far wider than the one printed. Its median,
0.183, lies well below its mean, so no lognormal can match the stated mean, interval and
confidence level at once. The 68\% reading may therefore correct a transposed digit, or it may
supply a width that stands in for a tail the lognormal lacks; the figure cannot distinguish the
two. Using the rescaled Bryson mixture itself as the $\eta_\oplus$ law matches Fig.~10 poorly
(Sec.~\ref{sec:variants}), probably because Bryson's published distribution is for a different
selection of planets and stars than the one Stark integrated over. We keep the 68\% lognormal and
treat the reading as an open question.

The redefined box enters only through $\rho_\mathcal{B}$, the ratio of the SAG13 integrals over
the two boxes, which does not depend on the normalization $\Gamma$. Its posterior comes from an
\texttt{emcee} chain over $(\ln\Gamma, \alpha, \beta)$ with SAG13 priors, and it is narrow because
$\Gamma$ cancels in the ratio.

\subsection{Exozodiacal dust}
\label{sec:exozodi}

Each star's exozodi level is drawn from the LBTI HOSTS maximum-likelihood distribution (Ertel et
al.\ 2020) that Stark et al.\ (2024) use, digitized from the vector data of their Fig.~9 (red
curve). One zodi is the dust level of the Solar System's own zodiacal cloud. The distribution has a
median of 2.98 zodis (Stark states three), and 21\% of draws lie above 100
zodis. The digitized histogram accounts for 0.948 of the $10^4 \times 500$ draws it
implies; we renormalize to make up the shortfall, which reproduces the stated median. As Stark
does, whenever levels are drawn we pin four stars to their LBTI measurements: $\epsilon$~Eri (297
zodis), $\theta$~Boo (148), 72~Her (588; listed as ``w~Her'' in HPIC) and 110~Her (235).

The heavy tail matters more than the median, because a star with hundreds of zodis is effectively
removed from the survey. The exozodi surface brightness follows Stark (2014, App.~C): a constant
optical depth in the habitable zone, evaluated at the Earth-equivalent insolation distance (EEID),
with solar colors.

\subsection{Albedo}
\label{sec:albedo}

Planets have geometric albedos drawn uniformly between 0.08 and 0.32 (Stark et al.\ 2024,
Sec.~3.2). Following Stark, the observation plan is fixed at $A_G = 0.2$, and a planet with a
drawn albedo is counted only if the fixed plan would have detected it: its albedo-adjusted flux
must lie inside the range of fluxes the visit actually reached. That range is bounded below by
the faintest flux the exposure detected and above by the brightest flux the inner working angle
allowed (``the faintest detected planet flux along the orbit segment \ldots\ is limited by the
exposure time, while the brightest planet flux along the segment \ldots\ is constrained by the IWA
of the coronagraph''). Both bounds are applied. Characterization is budgeted at $A_G = 0.2$, as
Stark (2024, Sec.~3.2) states.

With this test the albedo penalty on the expected yield is 3.6\%, against the 12\% that
Stark reports. The alternative of re-deriving each drawn-albedo planet's exposure time gives
8.6\%, so neither method reproduces the published value here
(Sec.~\ref{sec:variants}).

\subsection{Radiometry and exposure times}
\label{sec:radiometry}

Exposure times follow Eq.~1 of Stark et al.\ (2019),
\begin{equation}
\tau = (\mathrm{S/N})^2\,\frac{\mathrm{CR_p} + 2\,\mathrm{CR_b}}{\mathrm{CR_p}^2},
\end{equation}
where S/N is the required signal-to-noise ratio, $\mathrm{CR_p}$ is the planet's count rate, and
$\mathrm{CR_b}$ is the background count rate. The background includes leaked starlight, local
zodiacal light, exozodiacal light, and detector noise (dark current, and clock-induced charge with
the Geiger factor of 6.73 from Stark 2019, Eq.~9). Local zodiacal light follows Stark et al.\
(2014): the Leinert et al.\ (1998) spectrum, scaled by 0.69 to a solar elongation of 135$^\circ$
and adjusted for each star's ecliptic latitude. This matches the zodi in AYO's exposure-time
benchmark (Stark et al.\ 2025) to 0.93--1.01.

Both sky backgrounds are multiplied by the extended-source throughput $T_{\rm sky}$ of Stark
(2019, Eqs.~5--6), ``the instrument's throughput for extended sources'', computed ``by first
convolving the spatially-dependent PSF at all locations with a normalized uniform background''.
This is a function of position: background light near the inner working angle (IWA) is attenuated
by the same focal-plane mask that attenuates a planet there. Stark's map is not published, so we
reconstruct it as $T_{\rm sky}(r) = \Upsilon_{\rm c}(r)/\mathrm{EE}(0.7\,\lambda/D)
= 1.474\,\Upsilon_{\rm c}(r)$, where $\mathrm{EE}(0.7\,\lambda/D) = 0.678$ is the Airy
encircled energy inside the photometric aperture; it reaches 0.572 at large separation. This
assumes that the fraction of an
off-axis PSF falling inside the photometric core does not change with separation, and it omits the
$\sim\lambda/D$ smoothing of the convolution.

The coronagraph is the DMVC6 of Stark et al.\ (2024), digitized from the vector data of their
coronagraph figure. Its curves are evaluated in \emph{circumscribed} $\lambda/D$, the convention
the figure uses, and the collecting area is the full obscured primary mirror. Detection combines
the 450\,nm and 550\,nm channels in quadrature and requires S/N~7. The noise floor at
$\Delta\mathrm{mag} = 26.5$ enters as a systematic term in the exposure-time equation, which
lengthens exposures as a planet approaches it, rather than as a hard cut. The two-month limits on
detection and characterization include overheads (Stark et al.\ 2024, Table~2). Characterization
is an $R = 140$ spectrum from the integral field spectrograph (IFS), at the S/N per spectral bin
given in Stark's Fig.~3, using whichever of nine bandpasses is best, with 96 pixels per spectral
bin at 1\,$\mu$m, taken at the planet's most favorable orbital phase.

\textbf{No calibration.} The model contains no throughput factor fitted to Stark's results. His
22.5 candidates are an output of AYO, not an input, and a scalar fitted to them would absorb any
error that changes exposure times uniformly: at a refitted factor, every alternative treatment we
tested changes per-planet exposure times by a nearly uniform $1.0 \pm 0.05$, and the yield's weak
dependence on throughput (an elasticity of about 0.4) lets a fitted factor cancel it entirely. For
reference, the factor that would reproduce 22.5 is 1.20; step A03 records it as a
diagnostic only.

\subsection{Completeness and the survey allocation}

For each star, 1\,200--3\,000 planets are injected across the box and propagated through the
radiometry at up to six independent visit times. The completeness $C_i(\tau, k)$ is the fraction
of injected planets that are detected within exposure $\tau$ on at least one of $k$ visits
\emph{and} can be characterized within two months; Stark et al.\ (2019) state that planets failing
either limit ``did not count toward the yield''. The survey then maximizes
$\sum_i \eta_\mathcal{B} C_i$ within a two-year budget. Each star is charged
$k(\tau'\tau + t_{\rm oh})$ for detection, plus
$\eta_\mathcal{B} C_i\,(\tau'\bar{\tau}_{\rm char} + t_{\rm oh})$ for characterizing whatever it
finds, where $\tau'$ is the time multiplier for wavefront control and $t_{\rm oh}$ is the slew and
wavefront-control overhead per observation. Pooling every $(k, \tau)$ option into a single concave
envelope and sweeping the equal-slope multiplier $\lambda$ reproduces an exact
dynamic-programming optimum to 0.00\%.

\subsection{Why the yield ratio exceeds the occurrence ratio}
\label{sec:ratio}

The occurrence ratio, $0.204$, splits into two factors: about 0.32 from narrowing the
habitable zone and about 0.63 from selecting by mass. The yield ratio, $0.232$, is larger, for
three reasons, each visible as an edge in Fig.~\ref{fig:pgm}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Characterization time is freed} ($\eta_\mathcal{B} \to \lambda$). Fewer expected
detections leave more of the budget for searching. This raises the ratio.
\item \textbf{The faintest planets are removed} ($\mathcal{B} \to \mathbf{o}_{ij}$). Cutting the
zone off at 1.20 scaled AU removes planets up to $(1.67/1.20)^2 \approx 1.9$ times fainter than
those that remain. This also raises the ratio.
\item \textbf{Distant stars are lost} ($\mathcal{B} \to C_i$). Around distant stars, only the outer
part of the habitable zone lies outside the inner working angle, and that is the part the
redefined box drops. This lowers the ratio.
\end{enumerate}
The first two effects win. Scaling the canonical yield by the occurrence ratio alone would be
wrong by 13\%. Even so, under the redefined selection a 6\,m telescope reaches 25
candidates with a probability of only 1.20\%: with a narrow habitable zone and a
mass-selected sample, the decadal survey's benchmark is out of reach at this aperture for any
plausible value of $\eta_\oplus$.

\section{The characterization-time discrepancy}
\label{sec:open}

The yield level, the exozodi penalty, the aperture scaling of $P_{25}$ and the shape of the
realized-yield distribution all agree with Stark's (Sec.~\ref{sec:validation}). One discrepancy
remains, and it appears in two ways.

\textbf{Characterization times.} The mean characterization time of the first 18 EECs is
14.3 days at 6\,m and 2.05 days at 9\,m, against Stark's 22 and 3.5 (his
Sec.~4.1); the ratio between the two apertures is 7.0 against his 6.2. The distributions
show where the difference lies (Fig.~\ref{fig:pair14}). At every aperture, Stark's first 18 EECs
include a plateau of spectra taking 15--50 days, whereas in this model the individual
characterization times fall away steeply, with almost none above about 15 days at 9\,m.

\textbf{The target mix.} Compared with Stark's Fig.~11 (our Fig.~\ref{fig:pair11}), using the same
characterization-gated completeness and one representative exozodi draw, this model agrees for the
nearest stars: within 6\,pc the median completeness is 0.98 against a published
0.95. Further out the two diverge: stars at 10--15\,pc have a median of 0.35
(published 0.73), and stars at 15--20\,pc 0.07 (0.28). By luminosity, stars
of $1$--$2\,L_\odot$ have 0.08 (published 0.62), and stars of $2$--$5\,L_\odot$ have
0.14 (0.47), with 42\% of the latter allocated essentially no time. This
model's survey concentrates on nearby stars instead, and reaches Stark's total yield by completing
them more thoroughly.

\textbf{The mechanism.} At $R = 140$ near 1\,$\mu$m, the background during characterization is
dominated by exozodi. The exozodi count rate is set by a surface brightness that is nearly the same
for every star, while the planet dims as $d^{-2}$, so characterization time grows roughly as $d^4$.
For an Earth twin at quadrature, the ratio of exozodi counts to planet counts per spectral bin is
1.3 at $\tau$~Cet, 7 at 61~Vir, 20 at $\theta$~Boo and 36 at
110~Her, and the corresponding characterization times are 0.21, 10,
72 and 255 days, against a 60-day cap. Distant, luminous targets are priced out
of the survey, while nearby ones are characterized in hours to days.

AYO's own exposure-time calculator agrees with these times. The Stark et al.\ (2025) benchmark
gives Earth-twin water spectra of 37 days at 12.3\,pc and 104 days at 18.2\,pc, for a telescope
slightly better than the baseline, and given that benchmark's inputs our code reproduces its
per-observation times to 0.94--1.02. The exposure-time equations are therefore not where the two
models differ.

\textbf{Explanations ruled out by direct test.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The noise floor.} Removing it entirely leaves the completeness of luminous stars
unchanged.
\item \emph{The detector saturation definition.} Stark (2019) sets the saturation rate
$\mathrm{CR_{sat}}$ to ten times an Earth twin's count rate in its core pixel, whereas this model
uses the whole scene. Switching definitions changes characterization times by less than 1\%.
\item \emph{The number of IFS pixels.} Our 96 per spectral bin matches Stark (2024, Table~2).
\item \emph{Detector noise in general.} Stark's Scenario~D (a Skipper CCD) shortens
characterization times by a factor of 1.5, which his text attributes mostly to a 30\% gain in
throughput. The share left for the detector, about 12\%, matches this model's.
\item \emph{Which targets count as the first 18.} Ordering targets by survey priority rather than
by characterization cost changes the mean by 1\%.
\item \emph{The metric used for Fig.~11.} Detection-only completeness fixes the low-luminosity stars
but not the luminous ones.
\item \emph{Dust color.} The zero point is a solar-colored source normalized in the $V$ band, the
treatment Stark (2015) describes.
\item \emph{Exozodi brightness at the planet's own separation.} Stark (2019, Table~1) notes that it
``varies with spectral type and planet-star separation''; evaluating it there rather than at the
EEID leaves the first-18 times essentially unchanged.
\item \emph{Charging characterization per star.} Budgeting spectra per star, as in Stark et al.\
(2015), moves the yield with $\eta_\oplus$ fixed the wrong way and leaves the target mix unchanged.
\item \emph{The orbital phase of the spectrum.} Taking spectra at the detection phase rather than
the most favorable one lengthens the times only part of the way.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{No throughput setting supplies the difference.} Four parameters act on throughput: a
factor on all bands ($\kappa$), a separate factor on the spectral bands only ($\kappa_c$), the
normalization of the coronagraph leak term, and the radial shape of $T_{\rm sky}$. Step A13
evaluates a 56-point design over that four-parameter box, recording at every point the
6\,m planning yield, the characterization times at 6 and 9\,m, and the Fig.~11 completeness by
distance (Fig.~\ref{fig:corner}).
\begin{itemize}
\item Across all 56 points, the first-18 characterization time at 6\,m never exceeds
16.9 days, against the published 22.
\item Of the 20 points that reproduce the published 6\,m yield to within 10\%, the
first-18 time spans only 7.0--11.6 days at 6\,m and
0.79--1.49 days at 9\,m, overlapping neither published value.
\item Constrained on the yield alone, the fit gives $\kappa = 1.39$ and
$\kappa_c = 1.12$, with a correlation of -0.80 between their logarithms, and forces
the 6\,m characterization time to 9.7 days (6.4--12.7).
\item The singular values of the local sensitivity are 31.72, 2.31, 1.50, 0.90. The first is
13.7 times the second, so these observables constrain essentially one combination of the
four parameters: the anticorrelation between $\kappa$ and $\kappa_c$.
\end{itemize}
Characterization time can be bought within this family only by giving up yield: when every
published observable is imposed at once, the fit pushes $\kappa_c$ onto the lower edge of its
allowed range and the yield falls to about 16.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Two questions for the AYO authors}

Both remaining disagreements reduce to questions of fact that the published description does not
settle.

\textbf{Question 1: how does the AYO baseline survey characterize its 10--20\,pc targets within
the two-month limit?} Stark's Fig.~11 shows habitable-zone completeness of 0.5--0.8 for these
targets, while AYO's own calculator gives their Earth-twin spectra 37--104 days, and no setting of
the throughput parameters reconciles the two (Sec.~\ref{sec:open}). Either those stars are
characterized under conditions more favorable than an Earth twin at maximum elongation, or the
completeness in Fig.~11 is not gated on characterization, or characterization is charged to the
budget differently from the way this model charges it. Whether the S/N per spectral bin refers to
a single bin or to a band average is a further detail the papers leave open.

\textbf{Question 2: is $\eta_\oplus = 0.26$ the mean or the median, and is $[0.12, 0.55]$ an 86\%
or a 68\% interval?} The text says mean and 86\%. The median-and-68\% reading reproduces Fig.~10,
and the stated reading does not (Sec.~\ref{sec:variants}); the quoted interval holds
59\% of its own source distribution.

These questions bear directly on mission design. The characterization budget sets the target mix,
and the target mix sets which stars need exozodi measurements in advance. The $\eta_\oplus$
interval sets the width of the yield distribution, and the width sets $P(Y \ge 25)$, the quantity
the decadal recommendation is written in. Neither discrepancy is known to be an error in AYO; each
is equally consistent with an error in this reimplementation that has not been found. What the
evidence supports is narrower: the published description does not determine these two quantities.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{$T_{\rm sky}(r)$ is reconstructed.} Stark's map comes from two-dimensional PSF
simulations that are not published, and this model rescales the azimuthally averaged
core-throughput curve instead. The term matters: with a constant $T_{\rm sky}$, the mean realized
yield falls from 22.4 to 19.2 (Table~\ref{tab:variants}). A different
reconstruction would shift the absolute yields, though hardly the yield ratio.
\item \textbf{The coronagraph is azimuthally averaged.} The digitized one-dimensional DMVC6 curves
stand in for two-dimensional maps.
\item \textbf{Exozodi draws are interpolated.} The 200 draws (Stark uses 500) are evaluated
on a 23-level grid, and the interpolation changes optimized yields by at most 0.24\%.
Stark also draws the HOSTS distribution itself from its posterior; he finds the effect negligible,
and it is not modeled here.
\item \textbf{The $\eta_\oplus$ distribution is reconstructed and departs from Stark's text}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:eta}). It drives 99.1\% of the remaining disagreement with Fig.~10 but
barely moves the yield ratio (Table~\ref{tab:variants}). The 86\% reading and the Bryson-mixture
law are available as options.
\item \textbf{$\alpha$ and $\beta$ are dominated by their priors.} The ratio $\rho_\mathcal{B}$
inherits the shape of SAG13 rather than coming from a new fit to Kepler DR25.
\item \textbf{There is no stray light from binary companions.} AYO includes it; for HIP~77052 it is
about 10\% of the background.
\item \textbf{The area fill factor (0.785) has no published source.} It moves the yield by about
$\pm$4\%.
\end{itemize}

%======================================================================
\section{Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item For a mass-selected planet sample ($0.5$--$2\,M_\oplus$) in a narrowed habitable zone
($0.96$--$1.20$~AU), the baseline 6\,m HWO mission would find a median of 4.3 candidates,
against 18.8 for the canonical selection. The yield ratio is
$0.232^{+0.027}_{-0.021}$ (68\% interval), larger than the occurrence ratio because the survey
reallocates the freed characterization time and the narrower zone drops the faintest planets.
\item The probability of finding at least 25 candidates falls from 35.1\% to
1.20\%, which puts the decadal benchmark out of reach at this aperture.
\item The yield ratio stays between 0.2201 and 0.2386 across seven model variants spanning both readings of
the $\eta_\oplus$ interval, both albedo methods, the presence or absence of the $T_{\rm sky}$ term,
and a different functional form for $\eta_\oplus$.
\item The reimplementation, built from the published description with nothing fitted to Stark's
results, agrees with 21 of 24 independent published comparisons and reproduces the
shape of the published yield distribution.
\item Characterization times depend more steeply on target difficulty and aperture than in AYO, and
no throughput setting removes the difference. The reading of the $\eta_\oplus$ interval accounts
for 99.1\% of the remaining disagreement with the published yield distribution. Stark's
per-visit albedo test gives a penalty of 3.6\% against his reported 12\%.
\end{enumerate}
