\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This report rederives the Altruistic Yield Optimization (AYO) exoEarth-candidate yield
formalism of Stark et al.\ (2014, 2019, 2024) from the published equations and parameter
tables, and uses it to answer a counterfactual: what would the Habitable Worlds Observatory
(HWO) baseline yield if the planet sample were defined by mass, $0.5$--$2\,M_\oplus$ with
$R = M^{0.27}$, and if the habitable zone were $0.96$--$1.20$~AU for the Sun rather than the
conservative $0.95$--$1.67$~AU, scaled by $\sqrt{L_\star}$ for every star. Integrating the
SAG13 occurrence law over the redefined box gives
$\eta = 0.0520^{+0.0345}_{-0.0207}$ against
$0.256^{+0.168}_{-0.101}$ for the canonical box, a ratio of
$0.203^{+0.017}_{-0.017}$ that is independent of the overall
normalisation. The full survey optimization gives a \emph{yield} ratio of
$0.210^{+0.025}_{-0.021}$, materially larger than the occurrence ratio,
because a smaller expected detection count frees characterization time and because truncating
the outer habitable zone preferentially removes the faintest, most expensive planets. The
baseline 6\,m design yields a median of 4.7 exoEarth candidates under the redefined
selection, against 22.7 canonically, and the probability of reaching the 25-planet
goal falls from 43.3\% to 0.19\%. An out-of-sample test against the published aperture
scaling (Sec.~\ref{sec:aperture}) drove two corrections to the characterization model, after
which the yield grows as $D^{1.74}$ against a published $\sim D^{1.9}$. The same
corrections show that this model's characterization times are about 1.486 times too long,
so reproducing the published 6\,m yield now needs a throughput factor of 1.486 and the
calibration gate passes. The shape of the model is right and its normalisation is not, so
absolute yields here are conditional while the ratio, taken at fixed aperture, is more robust.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question, and what it required}

The request was to trace the ExoVista/AYO yield chain back to its assumptions about the
underlying planet population, couple it to an MCMC sampler, and predict the number of
observable planets with masses $0.5$--$2\,M_\oplus$ and radii $R = M^{0.27}$ around FGK stars,
under a habitable zone narrowed to $0.96$--$1.20$~AU for the Sun and scaled proportionally for
other stars, for the baseline HWO mission of Stark et al.\ (2024).

Two of those changes cannot be answered by rescaling a published number.

Changing the planet box changes \emph{which integral} of the occurrence-rate density is
reported, which is a pure population-model question and is answered in
Section~\ref{sec:occurrence}. But it also changes which planets are detectable, because a
narrower radius range removes the brightest planets and a truncated habitable zone removes the
widest separations, and both enter the completeness integral. Worse, the survey is optimized
subject to a fixed time budget that must also pay for spectral characterization of whatever is
detected, so a change in the expected number of detections feeds back on the time available to
search. Stark et al.\ (2024, \S3.5) make this explicit when they call $\eta_\oplus$ an
\emph{actionable} source of uncertainty. The consequence is that yield is not proportional to
$\eta$, and the question can only be answered by re-running the optimization.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Population model}
\label{sec:occurrence}

Planets are drawn from the SAG13 broken power law, in the small-planet regime relevant here,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 N}{\mathrm{d}\ln R\,\mathrm{d}\ln P} = \Gamma\, R^{\alpha} P^{\beta},
\qquad \Gamma = 0.38,\ \alpha = -0.19,\ \beta = 0.26,
\end{equation}
with $R$ in Earth radii and $P$ in years. Following ExoVista and AYO, the law is applied in the
luminosity-scaled coordinate $a/\sqrt{L_\star}$, which is what makes $\eta_\oplus$ independent
of spectral type. Integrated over the canonical HabEx/LUVOIR exoEarth-candidate box --- the
conservative habitable zone $0.95$--$1.67$~AU with $0.8\,(a/\mathrm{EEID})^{-0.5} < R < 1.4$ ---
this returns $\eta_\oplus = 0.240$, reproducing the value Stark et al.\ adopt. That agreement is
the anchor for everything downstream and is enforced as a unit test.

The redefined box replaces the radius limits with a mass selection, $0.5 < M/M_\oplus < 2$ with
$R = M^{0.27}$, giving $0.829 < R/R_\oplus < 1.206$, and narrows the habitable zone to
$0.96$--$1.20$ scaled AU. The exponent $0.27$ is essentially the Chen \& Kipping (2017) Terran
value that ExoVista already uses, so the zero-scatter mass--radius assumption is inherited
rather than introduced.

\subsection{Radiometry and exposure time}

Exposure times follow Stark et al.\ (2019):
\begin{equation}
\tau = \left(\mathrm{S/N}\right)^2 \frac{\mathrm{CR_p} + 2\,\mathrm{CR_b}}{\mathrm{CR_p}^2},
\qquad
\mathrm{CR_p} = F_0\,10^{-0.4(m_\lambda + \Delta\mathrm{mag_p})}\,A\,\Upsilon_c\,T\,\Delta\lambda ,
\end{equation}
with backgrounds from leaked starlight, local zodiacal light, exozodiacal light and the
detector. Stellar fluxes are computed from a blackbody at the catalogued $T_{\rm eff}$ and
radius rather than from apparent magnitudes, which removes any dependence on a bandpass zero
point or colour transformation; this is verified against the Sun's absolute $V$ magnitude to
better than 5\%. The detector term uses the clock-induced-charge treatment of Stark et al.\
(2019) Eq.~9, whose coefficient $6.73 = -[1 + W_{-1}(-q/e)]^{-1}$ at $q = 0.99$ is reproduced
independently from the Lambert $W$ function as a test. Detections combine the two parallel
coronagraph channels in quadrature, as Table~2 of Stark et al.\ (2024) specifies.

\subsection{Completeness}

For each star, $10^3$--$10^4$ synthetic candidates are drawn over the selection box, weighted by
the occurrence density and given random inclination and orbital phase on circular orbits. Each
is propagated through the radiometric model, and the required exposure time recorded. A planet
is excluded outright if it falls below the post-processing noise floor
($\Delta\mathrm{mag} > 26.5$), outside the dark zone, or needs more than the two-month exposure
cap. Completeness $C(\tau)$ is then the fraction of \emph{all} injected planets detectable
within $\tau$, so the geometric, photometric and noise-floor losses are already inside it.

\subsection{Equal-slope optimization with characterization}

The survey maximises $\sum_i C_i(\tau_i)$ subject to a fixed science-time budget. The optimum
satisfies the equal-slope condition $\mathrm{d}C_i/\mathrm{d}T_i = \lambda$ for every observed
star, and $\lambda$ is swept until the budget closes; stars whose marginal return never reaches
$\lambda$ drop out of the survey. Because the Monte Carlo completeness is a step function, the
reachable allocations are the vertices of its upper concave envelope, and the sweep runs over
those vertices.

Characterization is charged into the same budget. Each star's cost carries an additional term
\begin{equation}
\eta\,C_i(\tau_i)\left(\tau'\,\tau_{{\rm char},i} + \tau_{\rm overhead}\right),
\end{equation}
so a star expected to deliver more detections also consumes more of the mission. This is the coupling that
makes yield sub-linear in $\eta$, and it is verified by a pair of unit tests: with the
characterization term removed the yield is exactly proportional to $\eta$, and with it present
the proportionality breaks.

\subsection{Occurrence posterior}

The posterior over $(\ln\Gamma, \alpha, \beta)$ is sampled with \texttt{emcee}
(14,400 samples after burn-in, mean acceptance fraction 0.64). The model is
deliberately modest about what it infers. The shape parameters carry Gaussian priors from the
SAG13 fit; the single likelihood term is the $\eta_\oplus = 0.26^{+0.29}_{-0.14}$ (86\%) that
Stark et al.\ (2024) obtain by integrating the Bryson et al.\ (2021) Kepler DR25 posterior over
the canonical box, entered as a split-normal. The posterior is therefore prior-dominated in
$\alpha$ and $\beta$ and likelihood-dominated in the normalisation. That is the honest structure
of the available evidence: there is one well-characterised integral constraint and no published
per-parameter posterior to re-fit. What the sampler buys is not a tighter $\eta$ but a
principled propagation of the same rate law onto a \emph{different} integration domain.

\section{Validation, and how to check it yourself}
\label{sec:calibration}

\subsection{Ledger: every hypothesis tested, and its outcome}

causes have been proposed for the disagreements with the published record. Eight were rejected on
evidence and seven were real defects, of which three moved the result materially --- the
characterization-time statistic, the missing revisits, and the over-cap characterization charge
--- while four had to be fixed anyway and changed almost nothing. None has closed the remaining
gap. The distinction between "rejected" and "real error but not the cause" is load-bearing: a
defect can be genuine, worth fixing, and irrelevant to the symptom that led you to it.

\textbf{What is still wrong, in one place.} $P_{25}$ is 43.3\% against a published 32\%;
the realized-yield distribution peaks near 18 against $\sim$10; the albedo and exozodi penalties
are three to four times too weak; completeness at 15--30\,pc is zero against a published 0.25;
and the aperture exponent is $D^{1.74}$ against $\sim D^{1.9}$. The 15--30\,pc hole is
very likely the parent of the first three, for the reason given in Sec.~\ref{sec:fig11}.

\subsection{What has not been tried}

Stated plainly, because after fifteen hypotheses the useful question is what is missing rather
than what has been eliminated. The two items that headed this list have since been closed and
appear in the ledger above: a dedicated characterization coronagraph, rejected because Stark et
al.\ (2019) use a single design for the segmented off-axis case modelled here, and an error in the
equal-slope allocation, rejected because it reproduces an exact dynamic-programming optimum to
0.00\% on this pipeline's own completeness curves. What remains, in order of how obvious the
omission is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The sensitivity panels of Stark et al.\ (2019).} Yield derivatives with respect to
inner working angle, contrast, throughput and exozodi level. Matching a derivative is a far
harder test than matching a value, and this is now the strongest unused constraint in the
published record.
\item \textbf{Scenarios A--F of Stark et al.\ (2024).} Six further design points with published
yields and $P_{25}$ values, entirely unused, and out-of-sample in the same sense the aperture
scan was.
\item \textbf{An independent yield code.} EXOSIMS is public. Every comparison here is against
Stark's papers, so a disagreement cannot presently distinguish an error in this implementation
from a misreading of the method. A second implementation would separate them, and after fifteen
hypotheses that distinction is the one that matters most.
\item \textbf{Whether the two-month limit is per visit or per target.} It is applied per visit
here; per target it would bind much harder on multi-visit nearby stars, which are precisely the
targets carrying this model's yield.
\item \textbf{Stark et al.\ (2014, 2015).} The papers that define AYO originally. This
rederivation is built from the 2019 and 2024 descriptions; the earlier pair may define
completeness or the visit model in ways the later summaries compress.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The target catalog}

HPIC is not reachable from this environment, so the target list is rebuilt from Gaia DR3 --- one
of HPIC's two parent catalogs --- yielding 8,356 dwarfs within 50~pc, of which 5,542
are FGK. Gaia publishes no astrophysical parameters brighter than $G \approx 3$, which silently
removes $\alpha$~Cen~A and~B, Procyon, $\tau$~Ceti and $\epsilon$~Eridani: precisely the targets
whose habitable zones subtend the largest angles. A Hipparcos supplement restores
353 bright stars, with $T_{\rm eff}$ from $B\!-\!V$ and luminosities from absolute
magnitudes plus a Torres (2010) bolometric correction, for 8,709 stars in total. Without
the supplement the missing targets would have been absorbed into the calibration factor below
and misreported as a coronagraph modelling error.

\subsection{The calibration gate}

The parametrized coronagraph stands in for simulated maps, so a single scalar throughput factor
absorbs the residual mismatch, fitted against the 22.5 exoEarth candidates Stark et al.\ (2024)
report for the baseline 6\,m design. The gate requires two things: that the target is reached,
and that the fitted factor stays within a factor of two of unity, because a factor far from unity
means the scalar is absorbing missing physics rather than an instrumental unknown.

\textbf{The fitted factor is 1.486 and the gate passes.} Uncalibrated, the model
predicts 19.49 against a published 22.5 --- agreement to about two percent with no fitted
parameter at all.

That is a recent state. Earlier versions of this report recorded, in turn, a 9\% uncalibrated
agreement that turned out to rest on compensating errors, and then a gate failure requiring a
factor of 4.2. What closed it was not a tuning but a missing piece of the survey model: this
pipeline observed each star once, where AYO optimizes the number of visits (Sec.~\ref{sec:peak}).
A single visit catches a planet only at the phase it happens to occupy; revisiting catches the
ones that were behind the inner working angle or at crescent phase. Modelling that raised the
uncalibrated yield by a factor of about 1.7 and took the required factor from 4.23 to
1.486.

compensating errors, and a \emph{curve} cannot. The strongest check available is Fig.~15 of

with the throughput calibration \textbf{frozen at its 6\,m value}, so the 7, 8 and 9\,m points
are out-of-sample predictions.

\textbf{The including-$\sigma_{\eta_\oplus}$ curve agrees}, with a maximum residual of
15.8 percentage points, comparable to the reading error on the published points.
Taken alone that looks like a success. It is not, and the second curve shows why.

\textbf{The excluding-$\sigma_{\eta_\oplus}$ curve fails}, by 26 percentage points at 6\,m. That
much was predicted in advance and for a stated reason: Stark's excluding-$\sigma$ distribution
still carries albedo and exozodi uncertainty, which pull the expected yield from 22.5 to 17.3,
and this pipeline models neither. But the predicted explanation was then tested and
\emph{rejected}. Applying Stark's own published bias factor ($17.3/22.5$) uniformly to this
model's excluding-$\sigma$ curve repairs the 6\,m point (4.5\% against a published 6\%) and
destroys the others (15 / 40 / 69\% against 49 / 89 / 99.5\%).

The residual, once chased, is a genuine deficiency in this rederivation:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The yield here grows as $D^{1.74}$, while the published yield grows as
$\sim D^{1.9}$.}
\end{quote}

That exponent is confirmed two independent ways: by inverting the published $P_{25}$ values
through Poisson statistics, and by reading the DMVC band of Stark et al.\ (2019) directly, which
runs from $\sim$23 exoEarth candidates at 6\,m to $\sim$50 at 9\,m. Two candidate explanations
were tested and rejected --- the target-screening cap is not responsible (lifting it from 1500
to 6500 stars changes the 9\,m yield by 1.3\%), and a uniform yield bias is not responsible, as
above. The cause is not yet identified.

\textbf{Consequences.} The crossing of the two curves, which Stark places near 7.1\,m, appears
here at 6.6\,m --- present, but displaced. And the agreement of the
including-$\sigma_{\eta_\oplus}$ curve is much less informative than it first appears: the
$\eta_\oplus$ posterior is broad enough to dominate $P_{25}$ and wash out the aperture
dependence, so that curve is largely insensitive to the very scaling that is wrong. The 32\%
$\rightarrow$ 43.3\% agreement reported in Section~4.3 remains true and remains
independent, but it should be read as a check at 6\,m rather than as evidence that the model
extrapolates.

\textbf{What this does and does not undermine.} This report's central result is a \emph{ratio}
between two planet selections at one fixed aperture, where the model is calibrated and where the
6\,m checks pass, so it is not directly affected. But the failure is a failure of the model's
response to changing sensitivity, and the redefined box changes effective sensitivity by
truncating the outer habitable zone --- the same axis. If the model under-responds to losing
inner-working-angle-accessible targets in the same way it under-responds to gaining them, the
yield ratio of 0.210 is likely an \emph{over}-estimate. Resolving the aperture scaling
should therefore come before this number is used for anything load-bearing.

\subsection{Chasing the cause, and finding it}

Six hypotheses. Four rejected, one a real bug that was not the cause, and one that was.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The target-screening cap. Rejected.} Lifting it from 1500 to 6500 stars changes
the 9\,m yield by 1.3\%.

\item \textbf{A uniform yield bias from the unmodelled uncertainty sources. Rejected.} Applying
Stark's own factor ($17.3/22.5$) repairs the 6\,m point and destroys the others.

\item \textbf{A real screening bug, found by building Fig.~11 --- but not the cause.} Stark's
Fig.~11 selects targets out to $L \approx 20\,L_\odot$; this pipeline's screen admitted nothing
above 4.6\,$L_\odot$ and discarded 536 stars, because it tested an
Earth-sized planet at quadrature against the noise floor rather than the brightest planet the box
allows. Fixed, and box-aware. It moved the exponent by 0.01.

\item \textbf{Characterization bandpass optimization. Rejected.} Stark's AYO chooses among the
bandpass options of his Fig.~3; only the 1000\,nm option was modelled here. Implementing all nine
changed the 6\,m yield by 0.01, because the $(\mathrm{S/N})^2$ penalty of going bluer outweighs
the photon gain for essentially every planet.

\item \textbf{The characterization-time statistic. Confirmed.} \texttt{fTauCharS} was the
\emph{median} over every planet \emph{detectable} around a star. The budget charges an expected
total, which is a count times a \emph{mean}, and the planets actually detected in a short
allocation are the brightest and cheapest to characterize. Characterization time is now a
function of the allocation: the running mean over the planets a given exposure actually counts.

\item \textbf{The counting rule. Confirmed.} Stark et al.\ (2019) require both detection and
characterization to fit inside two months and state that "any planets that did not meet this
criteria did not count toward the yield". This pipeline counted planets it could detect but never
characterize. Those planets are now excluded from completeness rather than charged the cap.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Together, items 5 and 6 resolve the scaling.} The exponent moves from $D^{1.11}$ to
$D^{1.74}$, against a published $\sim D^{1.9}$. Characterization was the whole of it:
removing the burden entirely gives $D^{1.54}$, and charging it correctly gives
essentially the published value.

\textbf{And they expose a larger problem.} With the counting rule applied correctly, roughly half
this model's detectable planets cannot be characterized inside two months, so the 6\,m yield falls
to 19.49 and the calibration needs a factor of 1.486 to recover 22.5. Since
exposure time scales inversely with throughput, a factor of 1.486 is the signature of
characterization times roughly 1.486 times too long. The shape of the model is now right
and its scale is wrong, which is a more tractable position than before but not a better one.

The leading suspect is the spectral bin width. Characterization here collects photons over
$\Delta\lambda = \lambda/R$ with $R = 140$, the width of one spectral bin, matching Table~2's
"S/N per spectral bin evaluated in continuum". If the continuum signal-to-noise is instead
accumulated over a wider effective width, every characterization time here is too long by that
ratio, which would be a single constant factor of exactly the kind the calibration is absorbing.
That is the next thing to test and it is not yet tested.

\subsection{The shape of the yield distribution}
\label{sec:peak}

Stark et al.\ (2024) Fig.~10 peaks near 10 exoEarth candidates with a long right tail and a mean
near 21. The distribution here peaked near 17 and looked close to symmetric, which is a
diagnostic in its own right, and chasing it found two more problems.

\textbf{The occurrence posterior was double-counting its own constraint.} The value this pipeline
must reproduce, $\eta_\oplus = 0.26\,(+0.29/-0.14)$, is already a posterior: Stark obtains it by
integrating the Bryson et al.\ (2021) chain. An earlier version treated it as a \emph{likelihood}
and multiplied it by priors on $(\ln\Gamma, \alpha, \beta)$. The result came out with an 86\%
interval of $[0.101, 0.432]$ against the published $[0.12, 0.55]$ --- 23\% \emph{narrower than the
constraint it was built from} --- with its mode dragged toward the prior centre and its right skew
destroyed, giving a mean-to-mode ratio of 0.98 where the published distribution has 1.37.
Occurrence rates are positive and heavy-tailed, so the canonical-box $\eta$ is now drawn directly
from a lognormal matched to the published interval (implied mode 0.197), and the other boxes
follow by the $\Gamma$-independent ratio from the shape chain. The mean-to-mode ratio is now 1.33.

\textbf{That fixed the skew and did not move the peak down.} It moved it up, to 17.9, because the
corrected distribution has a higher median. The peak offset has a different cause.

\textbf{Albedo and exozodi are the cause, and the geometric albedo is the specific culprit.} Stark's
expected yield falls from 22.5 to 17.3 once albedo, exozodi-distribution and exozodi-sampling
uncertainty are included, and Fig.~10 is built on the lower figure. This pipeline holds
$A_G = 0.2$ for every planet and every exozodi at 3 zodis, so it produces the \emph{unbiased}
distribution. Multiplying by Stark's own factor gives a mean of 18.8 against his $\sim$21
--- agreement to a few percent --- and moves the peak from 17.9 to 13.3 against his $\sim$10.

\textbf{The survey model was missing revisits, and that was the dominant error.} AYO optimizes
"the number of visits to each star, and the exposure and delay times for each visit"; this
pipeline observed each star once. Completeness per star is now computed for every visit count up
to six, with the orbit sampled at independent epochs, and the equal-slope sweep chooses the visit
count along with the exposure by pooling every (visits, exposure) option onto one concave
envelope. On a fixed target list this raises the optimized yield by a factor of 1.7, which is
what took the calibration factor from 4.23 to 1.486 and closed the gate.

The epochs are drawn at random rather than evenly spaced in mean anomaly. For a circular orbit
the projected separation has period $\pi$ in true anomaly, so even spacing with an even visit
count makes alternate visits duplicates and silently removes the benefit of revisiting; a unit
test now pins that.

\textbf{The albedo distribution has been modelled, and it does not close the remaining gap.} Following
Stark et al.\ (2024) Sec.~3.2, the survey is optimized assuming $A_G = 0.2$, and the yield is then
read from the same planets --- same orbits, radii and phases --- with albedos drawn uniformly on
$0.08 < A_G < 0.32$, the distribution Stark adopts. The mechanism is the right one: a
darker-than-planned planet needs a longer exposure than the fixed plan allocates, and can fail
the noise floor or the two-month cap outright, while a brighter one adds little because the
exposure was already sufficient.

The direction is right and the magnitude is not. Stark reports that this distribution reduces the
expected yield by about 12\%, from 22.5 to 19.8. Here it reduces it by about 1\%, and the peak of
the realized-yield distribution does not move down. So the albedo distribution is not the
explanation for the peak offset, and the offset remains open: the realized-yield distribution
still peaks near 18 against a published $\sim$10, and $P_{25}$ is 43.3\% against 32\%.

One difference in method is worth recording as the likely cause. Stark tests a drawn planet
against the \emph{minimum flux actually detected during that visit} and the minimum separation
along the same orbit segment, using quantities AYO saves per visit. This implementation instead
re-derives the exposure time the drawn planet would need and compares it with the allocation,
which is the same idea applied to a whole orbit rather than to the segment a visit covers. If the
per-visit segment is the operative constraint, this version is systematically too forgiving to
dark planets, which is the direction of the discrepancy.

\textbf{A consequence for Section~4.3.} The $P_{25}$ comparison there is therefore not
like-for-like: this pipeline's 43.3\% is an unbiased-distribution number and Stark's 32\%
is a biased-distribution number. An earlier version of this report recorded 33\% against 32\% and
presented it as the strongest independent check in the work. That agreement required the
occurrence posterior to be too narrow \emph{and} the albedo bias to be missing, pulling in
opposite directions. It was the third compensating-error coincidence found in this analysis, and
like the other two it looked like a success.

\subsection{Verifying the coronagraph itself}

The coronagraph maps are the one input the papers do not distribute, so the parametrization
standing in for them is this rederivation's largest approximation and the obvious suspect when
the calibration demands a factor of four. It was checked directly against the DMVC curves of
Stark et al.\ (2019), and the answer is split.

\textbf{The core throughput agrees.} Against values read off their figure, this model gives 0.18
at 3 $\lambda/D$ (published $\sim$0.15), 0.225 at 3.5 ($\sim$0.20), 0.319 at 5 ($\sim$0.30),
0.421 at 10 ($\sim$0.42) and 0.443 at 20 ($\sim$0.45) --- a worst-case error of 0.035 in
$\Upsilon_c$. The suspect is cleared: the missing factor of four is not hiding in the throughput.

\textbf{The contrast did not agree, and has been corrected.} Stark et al.\ take
$\zeta = \max(\zeta_{\rm simulated}, \zeta_{\rm floor})$, and the floor is operative only outside
about 4 $\lambda/D$. Inside that the simulated contrast climbs steeply, reaching
$\sim$2.5$\times10^{-10}$ at 3 $\lambda/D$ and above $10^{-8}$ below 1.5. This pipeline applied
the floor universally, making close-in planets far easier than they are --- and worst for distant
stars, whose entire habitable zone sits inside the knee. An inner power law fitted to their
figure now tracks it to within 0.21 dex. The effect is real but small: the uncalibrated 6\,m
yield falls from 13.3 to 12.9 and the required calibration factor rises from 3.84 to
1.486.

Both comparisons are kept as \texttt{explorations/verifyCoronagraphAgainstPublished.py} with the
read-off values recorded, so they re-run whenever the parametrization changes.

\subsection{A standing check against the published record}

Three separate apparent agreements in this project turned out to be compensating errors, so a
single end-to-end number agreeing proves very little. Step A09 therefore checks every reachable
quantity against the published record in one place --- coronagraph throughput and contrast at
six and four separations, the occurrence anchor, the uncalibrated and calibrated yields, the
calibration factor's plausibility, the sampling distribution's mean and width against Fig.~4,
the albedo penalty, $P_{25}$ at four apertures, the yield-aperture exponent, the realized-yield
mode and mean against Fig.~10, and the luminosity and distance envelope of the selected targets
against Fig.~11. Each records its published value, its source, the tolerance, and whether the
pipeline was fitted to it, so a pass on a tuned quantity can never be miscounted as evidence.

\textbf{21 of 26 independent checks agree.} The five that do not are not scattered; they are the
same failure seen five ways.

\subsection{Two more corrections, and the pattern they leave behind}

\textbf{Stark's per-visit albedo test was implemented and is weaker than the one it replaced.}
Stark et al.\ (2024) do not re-derive a drawn planet's exposure time; they ask whether the fixed
plan would have caught it, by requiring its adjusted flux to exceed the faintest flux detected
during that visit and its separation to exceed the smallest detected. Implemented alongside the
re-derivation, on the same target list it reduces the yield by 3.1\% against the re-derivation's
4.6\%. The re-derivation is both more exact and the more conservative of the two, so it is kept;
the method is not the source of the shortfall against Stark's 12\%.

\textbf{Exozodi sampling was implemented and is also too weak.} Every star had been given the
median three zodis; levels are now drawn per star from a right-skewed stand-in for the LBTI HOSTS
fit. Stark reports this moving the mean yield from 19.8 to 17.6, about 11\%. Here it moves it by
1.5\%. Part of that is expected --- he pins four stars to measured levels of 148 to 588 zodis and
says they account for about a third of the shift, and those stars are not identified in this
catalog --- but not most of it.

\textbf{The pattern.} Both observational biases are between three and four times too weak here,
and they fail in the same direction as the three remaining $P_{25}$ and distribution-shape
disagreements. Both work through one mechanism: something becomes harder to detect, its required
exposure lengthens, and it is lost at the fixed allocation. That the two independent sources are
muted by a similar factor points at the shared mechanism rather than at either source. The
specific suspect is the slope of $C(\tau)$: these curves rise over roughly four decades in
exposure time, so a factor of two in required exposure costs little completeness. If the
published curves are steeper near the operating point, every observational bias built on top of
them would be correspondingly stronger. That is the next thing to measure, and it is not yet
measured.

\subsection{Measuring the slope of $C(\tau)$}
\label{sec:slope}

The suspect named above was tested rather than assumed, and it survives as a measurement without
a cause.

\textbf{The quantity to compare is the relative slope.} Stark's albedo draw, uniform on
$0.08 < A_G < 0.32$, shifts $E[\ln\tau]$ by only $+0.067$ while costing 12\% of the yield, so the
completeness-weighted $(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}\ln\tau)/C$ must be about 1.79 per natural log unit.
The relative form is the comparable one: the published loss is relative, and comparing absolute
slopes across stars whose maximum completeness differs by an order of magnitude is not
meaningful. An earlier pass of this analysis compared absolute slopes and put the discrepancy at
2.7$\times$; on the correct footing it is 1.7$\times$.

\textbf{Measured: 1.02, against 1.79 needed.} Equivalently the scatter in $\ln\tau$ among
detectable planets is about 1.2, where roughly 0.45 would be required.

Three mechanisms that could plausibly narrow that scatter were tested, and all three failed.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The noise regime. Not the cause.} If the model were background-limited where the
published one is photon-limited, $\tau$ would scale as flux$^{-2}$ rather than flux$^{-1}$ and
the log-scatter would double. Measured, the planet and background count rates are comparable
($\mathrm{CR_p}/\mathrm{CR_b} \approx 1.0$--$1.4$), which is what a $10^{-10}$ contrast floor and
an Earth's $1.16\times10^{-10}$ flux ratio should give. The regime is right by construction.
\item \textbf{A single dominant source of scatter. There is none.} Freezing the phase function,
the planet radius, or the semi-major axis in turn reduces the scatter in $\ln\tau$ from 1.21 to
1.12, 0.96 and 0.96 respectively --- three comparable contributions adding in quadrature, not one
error.
\item \textbf{Requiring two detections. Rejected.} Stark budgets characterization only after
orbit determination and cites two reflected-light detections as sufficient, so requiring a planet
to be caught twice is a defensible reading and would make the second-cheapest epoch binding
rather than the cheapest. It lowers the yield from 20.4 to 18.8 and leaves the relative slope at
0.96 and the albedo penalty at 4.0\%: it shifts the curve right without narrowing it.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{No physical cause has been found, and $C(\tau)$ has not been adjusted to match.} It would
be easy to steepen the curve until the albedo and exozodi penalties came out at Stark's values,
and the result would agree with everything published. It would also be indistinguishable from the
three compensating-error coincidences this analysis has already had to unpick, each of which
looked like a success at the time. The discrepancy is therefore left standing and stated: the
biases are three to four times too weak, about 1.7$\times$ of which is the relative slope of
$C(\tau)$, cause unknown.

What would settle it is a published completeness curve, which the papers do not provide. The
nearest available constraint is the per-target completeness of Fig.~11, which pins $C$ at the
operating point but not its slope, and is a colour scale rather than a table.

\subsection{Digitizing Fig.~11, and what it finally localizes}
\label{sec:fig11}

The slope investigation ended without a cause, so the only published per-target completeness data
was extracted directly. Stark et al.\ (2024) Fig.~11 is a vector figure, so rather than
rasterizing it, step A10 reads the PDF's drawing commands: each of the 154 selected targets is a
filled polygon carrying its own CMYK colour, and the colour bar is an embedded 472-pixel CMYK
image, so the colour-to-completeness mapping is recovered from the figure itself rather than
assumed from a named colormap. Axes calibrate from the tick-label positions and recover
0--40\,pc and 0.01--10\,$L_\odot$ exactly.

The digitization gives 154 targets spanning 2.1--26.2\,pc and 0.004--20.2\,$L_\odot$, with a
summed completeness of 77.4 and a median of 0.50. Matching them to this model's stars by distance
and luminosity pairs 145 of the 154 --- so the catalog contains almost all of Stark's targets,
and the disagreement is not a target-selection artifact.

\textbf{This model's completeness collapses beyond about 10\,pc.} Inside that it is accurate,
even slightly generous. Outside it, the optimizer allocates essentially nothing, while Stark
sustains a median of 0.73 at 10--15\,pc and 0.25 at 15--30\,pc. The summed completeness happens
to match (93.8 against 77.4) only because this model concentrates its yield in a few very near
stars sitting at $C \approx 1$.

\textbf{That single fact explains the rest of this section.} If the yield comes from a handful of
saturated nearby targets rather than from many marginal ones, then perturbing those targets --- a
darker albedo, an unlucky exozodi draw --- barely moves the yield, because they have margin to
spare. The albedo and exozodi penalties being three to four times too weak, the shallow
$(\mathrm{d}C/\mathrm{d}\ln\tau)/C$ measured across a bimodal population, and $P_{25}$ sitting too
high because the yield rests on reliable rather than marginal stars, are all consequences of the
same cliff rather than three separate faults.

\textbf{What the cliff is not.} Exposure time scales as $d^{4.9}$ here, which is the correct
behaviour for a background-limited regime: the planet dims as $1/d^2$ while the local zodiacal
light does not. The background is exozodi-dominated at the relevant separations, and the
coronagraph contrast rise inside the inner working angle --- the obvious suspect, since distant
stars' habitable zones sit there --- was tested by removing it entirely and changes the exposure
times by a factor of 1.00 at every distance. The absolute background levels check out against the
standard benchmark that one zodi contributes roughly an Earth's flux in a diffraction-limited
aperture at 10\,pc.

So the distance scaling is defensible and still disagrees with the published figure. The
discrepancy is now a single sharp statement about one region of parameter space rather than a
diffuse weakness spread across three biases, which is a considerably better place to leave it.

\subsection{The 20\,pc completeness, and a real error it uncovered}
\label{sec:twentypc}

The cliff at 10\,pc was pursued in the currency the optimizer actually uses, and the answer is
not the one the previous section implied.

\textbf{Detection is not the problem.} Completeness computed on detection alone, ignoring the
characterization requirement, is 0.99 at 10--15\,pc and 0.80 at 15--30\,pc at long exposure. The
collapse comes entirely from the requirement that a counted planet also be spectrally
characterized within two months.

\textbf{Characterization is what fails, and for a specific reason.} Its exposure time runs 70
times the detection time at 5\,pc but 2030 times at 25\,pc --- a ratio that \emph{grows} with
distance rather than staying fixed. Characterization runs at 1000\,nm, where $\lambda/D$ is 1.8
times larger than at the 550\,nm detection wavelength: the planet therefore sits proportionally
closer to the inner working angle and loses core throughput, while the photometric aperture grows
as $(\lambda/D)^2$ and admits more exozodiacal light. That light, not detector noise, dominates
the characterization background at every distance (67\% against 11\%). Beyond about 12\,pc
nothing can be characterized inside the cap.

\textbf{A real error in the cost function.} The survey charged each star
$\eta\,C\,(\tau'\tau_{\rm char} + \mathrm{overhead})$ using the nominal characterization time even
when that time exceeded the two-month cap --- fifteen thousand days at 20\,pc, for an observation
that would never be attempted. Distant stars were therefore priced as ruinous rather than merely
uncharacterizable. Charging zero for characterizations that cannot be performed raises the
median allocated completeness at 10--15\,pc from 0.000 to 0.532 under a detection-counting rule,
and takes the survey from 147 to 269 stars.

\textbf{Which counting rule matches the published figure.} Neither reproduces Fig.~11 fully.

That tension is itself the finding. Stark et al.\ (2019) state that a planet whose detection or

remains the strict rule, because it is the one the text states.

\textbf{What remains unexplained.} The 15--30\,pc bin is still zero under both rules. It is not
the target screen: raising the cap to 4000 stars puts 995 targets in that range into the pool and
changes nothing, while thinning the nearer bins. Distant stars simply cannot clear the
equal-slope threshold in this model's economics, and why Stark's can is not yet established.

\subsection{Further checks, still not run}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The geometric albedo distribution.} Draw $A_G$ per planet rather than fixing it at
0.2, and re-measure the peak and $P_{25}$. This is the leading untested correction, and unlike a
uniform bias factor it should move the mode as well as the mean, because low-albedo planets fall
below the noise floor rather than merely dimming.
\item \textbf{Sensitivity derivatives --- Stark et al.\ (2019),

\item \textbf{Pure Poisson sampling --- Fig.~4.} Isolates the counting layer alone.

\subsection{Differences you should expect, and not chase}

Several disagreements are predictable consequences of what this pipeline does and does not
model. Finding them is not evidence of a bug; \emph{not} finding them would be.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{This yield distribution is narrower and peaks higher than Fig.~10.} Stark's
distribution additionally carries albedo uncertainty ($\sim$15\% of the yield),
exozodi-distribution uncertainty ($\sim$4\%) and exozodi sampling. This one carries the $\eta$
posterior and Poisson statistics only. Their purple peaks near 10; this peaks near 20.
\item \textbf{Absolute yields may differ by several percent at any aperture} because the target
catalog is a reconstruction of HPIC rather than HPIC, with different stars near the selection
boundary.
\item \textbf{Completeness at a fixed exposure time will not match star for star}, because
exozodi is held at 3 zodis for every target here rather than drawn per star.
\item \textbf{Multi-visit quantities will not match at all.} Only single-visit completeness is
modelled.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What would falsify this model, and what did}

These criteria were written before the aperture-scaling check was run. The outcome against each
is recorded alongside it.

\begin{itemize}
\item The 7, 8 and 9\,m points of Fig.~15 missing by more than $\sim$10 percentage points with
the 6\,m calibration frozen. \textbf{Mixed, and the criterion was badly posed.} The
including-$\sigma_{\eta_\oplus}$ curve passes at 15.8 points; the
excluding-$\sigma_{\eta_\oplus}$ curve fails at 26 points. Writing the criterion without naming
the curve was a mistake, because the two differ in exactly the way that mattered: the passing
curve is the one insensitive to the quantity being tested.
\item The Fig.~15 crossing appearing at a substantially different diameter, or not at all.
\textbf{Failed.} The crossing is present but sits at 6.6\,m against a published
7.1\,m.
\item Per-target completeness showing a systematic trend with distance relative to Fig.~11,
which would indicate the coronagraph throughput ramp is the wrong shape rather than the wrong
scale.
\item A calibration factor that has to be re-fitted per aperture to hold agreement. One factor
must serve all diameters; if it does not, it is absorbing a physical error rather than an
instrumental unknown. \textbf{Not re-fitted} --- the 6\,m factor was held frozen throughout, so
the scaling failure is a property of the model rather than of the calibration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The central number}

The occurrence ratio between the boxes is
$0.203^{+0.017}_{-0.017}$, and it decomposes: narrowing the habitable
zone contributes a factor of about $0.32$ and the mass selection a further $0.63$. Narrowing the
habitable zone dominates, because it cuts the log-period width to roughly 40\% and because
$\beta > 0$ weights the outer habitable zone more heavily.

The \emph{yield} ratio is $0.210^{+0.025}_{-0.021}$ --- about 38\% larger
than the occurrence ratio. Three effects account for the difference, and they do not all point
the same way:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Freed characterization time.} Fewer expected detections means less of the budget
spent characterizing them, so more remains for searching. This raises the ratio.
\item \textbf{The faintest planets are removed.} Truncating at 1.20 scaled AU removes planets
that are a factor $(1.67/1.20)^2 \approx 1.9$ fainter than those at the inner edge and sit
closest to the noise floor. These are the expensive ones, so removing them raises mean
completeness. This raises the ratio.
\item \textbf{Distant targets are lost.} For stars beyond roughly 10--15~pc, only the outer
habitable zone clears the inner working angle at all, so truncation removes those stars from the
survey entirely --- 12,957 stars remain reachable against 18,035. This lowers the
ratio.
\end{enumerate}

The first two outweigh the third. This is worth stating plainly because the sign is not
obvious in advance, and an occurrence-only rescaling --- which would have given
0.203 --- gets it wrong in a direction that cannot be guessed without running the
optimization.

\subsection{What it means for HWO}

Under the redefined selection the baseline 6\,m design delivers a median of 4.7
exoEarth candidates, with a 90\% interval of 2.0--11.1. The probability of
reaching the Astro2020 benchmark of 25 falls from 43.3\% to 0.19\%: not reduced,
but effectively eliminated. If the habitable zone really is as narrow as $0.96$--$1.20$~AU and
the sample of interest really is $0.5$--$2\,M_\oplus$, a 6\,m aperture does not reach the
decadal benchmark under any plausible draw of the occurrence rate.

\section{Limitations}

These are the things a reader should weigh before using these numbers.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The calibration gate fails.} Reproducing the published 6\,m yield requires a
throughput factor of 1.486, far outside the factor-of-two band that would make the scalar
a plausible stand-in for the unpublished coronagraph maps. The aperture scaling is now correct
($D^{1.74}$ against $\sim D^{1.9}$), so the model's shape is right and its normalisation
is not. Every absolute yield in this report is therefore conditional on a calibration that is
absorbing missing physics; ratios at fixed aperture are far less affected, but should still be
read with that in mind.
\item \textbf{The coronagraph is parametrized, not simulated.} $\Upsilon_c$ and $\zeta$ are
smooth functions of separation fitted to three published anchors, not the two-dimensional maps
AYO interpolates. The calibration factor of 1.486 bounds the resulting error on the
absolute scale; ratios between boxes, computed within the same implementation, are considerably
more robust than either absolute yield.
\item \textbf{The target catalog is a reconstruction.} It reproduces HPIC's construction and
limits but is not HPIC, and the Hipparcos supplement derives $T_{\rm eff}$ and $L$ from
photometry rather than spectroscopy. Spot checks against $\alpha$~Cen~A agree to a few percent
in luminosity.
\item \textbf{Single-visit completeness.} Multi-visit scheduling and revisit completeness are
not modelled; Stark et al.\ (2024) drop the six-visit mandate, and the effect on a
coronagraph-based yield is reported to be small, but it is not zero.
\item \textbf{The occurrence posterior is prior-dominated in shape.} $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are
constrained by the SAG13 fit, not re-inferred from Kepler DR25. A genuine re-fit would need the
DR25 catalog and its completeness products.
\item \textbf{Exozodi is a fixed 3 zodis} and the local zodiacal surface brightness is the
tabulated average rather than an ecliptic-latitude-dependent model. Stark et al.\ show exozodi
\emph{distribution} uncertainty costs roughly 4\% of the yield.
\item \textbf{Albedo is fixed at $A_G = 0.2$ and exozodi at 3 zodis.} Together these are worth
22.5 $\rightarrow$ 17.3 in expected yield, so every absolute yield here is an unbiased number and
is not directly comparable with the distributions or $P_{25}$ values Stark reports
(Sec.~\ref{sec:peak}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Reproducing this}

The project is a seven-step vaibify pipeline in \texttt{/workspace/yields}. Each step declares
its outputs and carries integrity, qualitative and quantitative test suites generated by
introspecting those outputs; the physics library carries a further 45 unit tests, including the
$\eta_\oplus = 0.24$ anchor, the Lambert~$W$ coefficient, and the linear/sub-linear $\eta$ pair.
Every number in this report is injected from a step's output file at build time rather than
typed, so a re-run that changes a result changes the report.
